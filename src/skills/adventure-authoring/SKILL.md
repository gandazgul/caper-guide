# Writing runnable adventures

An adventure package is a practical instrument for play, not a short story the players must reenact. Its
quality is measured by whether a Guide can understand the situation and respond consistently to player
decisions. Preserve the source's distinctive premise, imagery, relationships, and constraints; do not sand
every adventure into generic fantasy or automatically rewrite a linear source.

## Write situations the players can act on

For a scene, make clear where it is, what is happening, what can be perceived, what can be interacted with,
and what pressure or uncertainty matters. Draw these details from the source and approved adaptation. Write
what an NPC wants, knows, and can do rather than predicting exactly what the players will say. Represent
authored transitions as conditions, not inevitable story beats.

Supported actions are examples, not an exhaustive menu. Good choices differ in intent, risk, information,
time, resources, or relationships; three phrasings of the same action are not three meaningful choices. Do not
require the players to guess the author's intended verb. When the source gives only one route or an unclear
solution, flag the fragility and ask before adding routes, clues, or mechanics.

Retain important exceptions and interactions. “A dangerous room” is not an adequate substitute for what
triggers its hazard, what warning is visible, and what the authored consequence is. If the PDF does not
establish those facts, record the ambiguity and seek an approved interpretation instead of filling it in.

## Separate prose by audience and job

- Put stable world facts and constraints in canon. Keep each fact focused enough to cite and check.
- In an entity's player summary, include only generally observable, non-secret information. Put private
  identity, motives, knowledge limits, and behavior guidance in guide notes and the appropriate canon.
- Treat a scene summary as a Guide-facing description of the situation, not automatically safe read-aloud
  text. Keep secrets in their proper records and use secret IDs rather than casually exposing them.
- Put triggers, exceptions, costs, and resolution procedures in runtime policy or appropriate structured
  fields. Do not hide a critical rule inside an atmospheric sentence.
- Preserve stable IDs and consistent names. A future Guide should not have to guess whether two differently
  named doors or characters are the same thing.
- Describe changes in compact, reviewable pieces. Use adventure_read before revising an existing record, and
  the author tools to save it; attractive prose in chat is not a durable artifact.

## Help a beginner make an informed choice

Explain what a setup choice changes at the table. “A rules-light game” needs a practical explanation such as
fewer numbers to track and more decisions made by interpreting the situation. Give real tradeoffs, not one
attractive recommendation surrounded by obviously bad alternatives.

Use ask_author for setup choices, with a recommendation grounded in the source's needs and the author's stated
preferences. Distinguish “the PDF says” from “I recommend” and “you chose.” Source citations can explain
context; they cannot establish someone's comfort boundaries or turn a proposed rule into a published rule.
Preserve free-text answers faithfully instead of silently converting them to your favorite option.

## Editing pass before saving or declaring readiness

Check whether another Guide could run the material without this chat: are actors, references, triggers,
knowledge boundaries, and consequences understandable? Does each claimed source fact have appropriate
evidence? Are choices still the players' to make? Is the opening situation immediately playable? Remove
redundant lore and prose that adds atmosphere but no useful orientation. Run adventure_validate after
meaningful edits, but do not confuse structural validation with proof of good writing or source fidelity.

Illustrative design distinction, not canon: “The players befriend the keeper, learn the password, and enter”
predetermines their actions. “The keeper knows the password and wants the missing ledger returned” describes
an actionable situation—but only use that version if those motives and facts are established in the package.

Background: Justin Alexander's
[Don't Prep Plots](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/4147/roleplaying-games/dont-prep-plots) explains
situation-based preparation. Here that principle is bounded by the existing adventure and its adaptation
policy; it is not permission to invent a larger world.

# Clear prose

Good writing helps this reader notice, understand, or decide something. Literary flourish is useful when it
serves that purpose; length and elaborate vocabulary are not evidence of quality. Match the adventure's chosen
tone and the user's preferences rather than imposing a single house voice.

## Choose what the paragraph does

Before writing, identify its reader and job: orient a player, explain a rule, establish a character, expose a
choice, or help a future Guide run a scene. Lead with what that reader needs now. Keep background that does
not affect the current question in the relevant asset instead of delivering a lore lecture.

Use specific nouns and direct verbs. Prefer an observable detail over an adjective announcing its effect:
describe the evidence of decay or danger instead of repeatedly calling things ancient, eerie, or ominous. One
well-chosen sensory detail can carry more atmosphere than a list of every sense. Do not manufacture objects,
sounds, motives, or clues merely to improve a sentence; use only authorized details.

Vary sentence length with purpose. Short sentences can clarify a consequence or quicken an exchange; a longer
sentence can connect related details. Avoid turning every response into the same three-sentence template. Use
active voice when it makes responsibility clear, but passive voice is useful when the actor is unknown or
irrelevant.

## Remove friction, not personality

- Delete filler introductions, repeated recaps, stock intensifiers, and redundant explanations.
- Prefer one strong image to several competing metaphors. Avoid habitual portentous endings and repeated
  constructions such as “not merely X, but Y.” Do not ban a word when it is genuinely the best choice.
- Keep names, locations, pronouns, and timing unambiguous. Repeat a name when “it” or “they” could mislead.
- Use plain language to teach a new player. Define an unfamiliar term at the moment it matters, with a short
  example if useful; do not explain every convention on every turn.
- Let straightforward information remain straightforward. “Show, don't tell” is not a reason to obscure a
  rule, an exit, a cost, or what the player can perceive.
- Make dialogue sound like someone trying to achieve something, not a narrator distributing exposition.
- Use lists for choices and operational notes; use prose for a moment in the fiction. Avoid heavy formatting
  that makes an ordinary exchange feel like a report.

## Revise for meaning before polish

Check in this order: Is it true to the relevant source/state? Can this audience know it? Is its purpose clear?
Is it specific enough to be useful? Can anything be removed without losing meaning or voice? Fix an unclear
situation before decorating it. Do not remove a qualification that distinguishes a known fact from an
inference. Do not replace a precise game term with a “fresh” synonym that changes its meaning.

Illustrative edit only, not adventure canon: given an authorized fact that a barred door has a gap admitting
light, replace “A profoundly mysterious portal radiates an ineffable invitation” with “Light shows beneath the
barred door.” The revision offers something the player can understand and investigate without telling them how
to feel or inventing what lies beyond it.

Background:
[GOV.UK clear-language guidance](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)
supports reader-centered clarity. Apply that clarity to explanations; fiction may still have a distinctive
voice.
